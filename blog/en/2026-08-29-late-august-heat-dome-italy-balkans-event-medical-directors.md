Just as the trail and marathon calendar traditionally shifts toward autumn, a new heat dome is pushing temperatures above 40°C across Italy and the Balkans in late August 2026. With humidity driving the feels-like temperature close to 50°C and tropical nights that never drop below 24°C, this late-season wave is a reminder to event medical directors that exertional heat stroke risk does not follow the calendar.

## A heat wave that ignores the racing calendar

Available late-August forecasts show a heat dome extending across Italy and the Balkans, with highs topping 40°C again in several regions. Combined with high humidity, the feels-like temperature is running close to 50°C. A second compounding factor is at play: tropical nights, during which temperatures never fall below 24°C, denying the body the thermal recovery window it needs after a day of intense heat.

This is exactly the point in the season when Italy's racing calendar traditionally shifts toward autumn and winter, with October through April generally considered the most favorable months for distance events. That shift rests on an implicit assumption, that thermal risk fades along with summer. The late-August 2026 heat dome is a reminder that assumption no longer holds.

## Why the tropical night makes the risk worse for athletes

A single hot day is already a well-documented risk factor for exertional heat stroke. What sets an episode like late August 2026 apart is the absence of overnight relief. A runner training early in the morning, assuming they are taking advantage of cooler conditions, is in fact starting from an already elevated thermal baseline, since the body never had the chance to dissipate the heat accumulated the day before. Across several consecutive nights of tropical heat, that cumulative effect echoes the same physiological mechanisms documented earlier this summer during the Pentecost weekend of 2026, when Hyrox Lyon and the Paris Marathon exposed the limits of medical response to exertional heat stroke.

For events held in the Balkans, where the altitude and terrain of trails such as those following the Vjosa river or the Sharr mountains create an illusion of coolness, the same trap applies: exertion at altitude or in mid-mountain terrain does not automatically protect against thermal risk when the ambient air stays hot and humid in a valley bottom or over the first kilometers of a course.

## Cool First, Transport Second still applies outside the classic summer season

The international consensus reaffirmed by the IOC, World Athletics, and ASPETAR is consistent on this point: when exertional heat stroke is suspected in an athlete, cold water immersion must begin on scene, before transport, with the goal of bringing core temperature below 39°C within 30 minutes of collapse. Casa et al. (Exercise and Sport Sciences Reviews, 2007) measured this method's cooling rate at 0.35°C per minute, roughly ten times faster than wet towels or ice packs. The Korey Stringer Institute, tracking more than 401 cases treated under this protocol, reports a 100 percent survival rate when temperature drops below 40°C within that window.

For a medical director planning a late-summer or early-autumn event, the question is therefore not whether the risky season has ended, but whether the medical post has the right equipment on hand, regardless of the date on the calendar.
