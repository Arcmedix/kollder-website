While Germany was recording its deadliest heat season since 1992, with nearly 14,000 heat-related deaths, its fire services were confronting a vegetation fire danger index at its maximum level across several states. For Germany's Feuerwehr, the June 2026 heat wave was not only a public health story. It was a sustained operational campaign, in physiological conditions the country rarely associates with its own climate.

## A wildfire danger index at its highest level

By June 2026, the Forest Fire Danger Index (Waldbrandgefahrenindex, WGI) had reached level 4 across large parts of southern and eastern Germany. From 25 June, it climbed to level 5, the top of the scale, in parts of Lower Saxony, Brandenburg, and other states. The Grassland Fire Index (Grasland-Feuerindex, GFI) also reached level 4 across much of the country.

Industry feedback reflects the strain: nearly half of German firefighters report additional stress from operations conducted in extreme weather conditions, and 85 percent of personnel expect climate-related extreme weather operations to increase in the years ahead.

## A compounding physiological risk for German crews

A wildfire operation in France or southern Europe is well documented for combining radiant heat from the fire front, turnout gear that limits sweat evaporation, and sustained physical exertion. In Germany, that same risk profile was layered, between 22 and 28 June, onto ambient temperatures that themselves exceeded 40°C at 46 of the country's roughly 200 weather stations, with the night of 27 to 28 June recorded by the Deutscher Wetterdienst as the warmest on record.

For crews engaged on vegetation fires under these conditions, the exertional heat stroke risk factors stack directly on top of each other: extreme ambient heat before deployment even begins, additional thermal load from gear and exertion, and rotations that, as documented this season on major fires in Greece, Croatia, and Belgium's Hautes Fagnes, can run far longer than initially planned.

## The same protocol, a doctrine still taking shape in Germany

The **ACSM Expert Consensus Statement 2023** and IOC guidance (Hosokawa Y, Racinais S et al., BJSM 2021) are unambiguous: when exertional heat stroke is suspected in a responder, cold water immersion must begin on scene, before transport, with the goal of bringing core temperature below 39°C within 30 minutes of collapse. Casa et al. (Exercise and Sport Sciences Reviews, 2007) measured this method's cooling rate at 0.35°C per minute, roughly ten times faster than wet towels or ice packs. The Korey Stringer Institute, tracking more than 401 cases treated under this protocol, reports a 100 percent survival rate when temperature drops below 40°C within that window.

In a country where wildfire has historically ranked below flooding or storms as a fire service priority, thermal rehabilitation doctrine for responders remains less developed than in France's SDIS network or in fire services across southern Europe. The 2026 season is changing that equation. A vegetation fire danger index at its maximum level, combined with record ambient temperatures, has reproduced on German soil the same risk conditions typically documented much further south.
