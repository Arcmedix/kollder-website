Facing a tight budget, some churches consider renting rather than buying a portable baptistry. The right choice mostly comes down to one factor: how often the church expects to hold baptisms in the years ahead.

## What renting involves

**Advantages.** No large upfront investment, well suited to a church holding a one-off baptism or testing immersion practice for the first time.

**Limits.** Rental availability remains limited, which complicates booking a specific date. Cumulative rental costs across a few services often exceed the price of buying outright after just a handful of ceremonies. And a rented unit is never guaranteed available for a date set in advance, which forces the service to work around an outside schedule.

## What buying involves

**Advantages.** The equipment belongs to the congregation and stays available at any time, with no dependence on a third party. Over several years, cost per service drops as the equipment gets used, especially with a rigid structure built to last. A church can also lend its baptistry to a neighboring congregation that doesn't have one yet.

**Limits.** A higher upfront investment than a one-off rental, which means setting aside a dedicated budget in advance.

## The break-even point

In practice, once a church expects more than two or three baptisms over the coming years, buying becomes the more economical option, not counting the added flexibility: no booking to plan around, no dependence on a vendor's availability, a service scheduled on the congregation's own calendar rather than a rental company's.

That's why most equipped churches choose to buy rather than rent, once immersion becomes a lasting part of church life.
