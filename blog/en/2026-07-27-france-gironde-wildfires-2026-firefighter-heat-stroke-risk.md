Five consecutive nights. That is how long thousands of French firefighters have been fighting, without pause, the extraordinary wildfire burning through Gironde and the Landes since last Wednesday. Nearly 42,000 hectares have already burned. Behind the headline figures on scorched land, another number rarely makes the official briefings: how many of those firefighters crossed the threshold into exertional heat stroke during those nights.

## A season that started three weeks early

France's Prime Minister acknowledged it directly during the interministerial crisis cell convened in Marseille in early July: wildfires this year are arriving **roughly two to three weeks ahead of their usual schedule**. Nearly 7,000 fire outbreaks have already been recorded since the start of the season, with around 2,000 firefighters, soldiers, and civilian reinforcements mobilized every single day nationwide. More than 50,000 hectares have burned in France since January 1, three times the figure for the same period last year.

On the Gironde and Landes front, the situation escalated enough to trigger the EU Civil Protection Mechanism. Two Croatian Canadair aircraft, two Portuguese Air Tractors, and two heavy Czech and Slovak helicopters were deployed to reinforce national resources. Five firefighters were injured in the sector, homes were destroyed, and military units were brought in to help stop the fire's advance toward the Bordeaux metropolitan area.

## Why this scenario maximizes exertional heat stroke risk

A wildfire that burns for five consecutive nights is not a single spike of heat exposure. It is a cumulative stack of risk factors, shift after shift, all converging on the same physiological threat.

**Unpredictable engagement duration** is the first factor. A rotation planned for two hours can stretch into four or six when the fire front shifts direction without warning. On the Gironde fires, Meteo-France forecast a probable heatwave from Tuesday, with highs expected up to 40 degrees Celsius and drier winds, a combination that further complicates suppression efforts and extends crew exposure.

**Radiant heat from the flame front** compounds ambient temperature. Firefighters working near the head of a fire experience temperatures far above what weather stations record, even before the official heatwave has peaked.

**Multi-night fatigue accumulation** changes the equation compared to a single isolated call. A spokesperson for the Gironde fire department described crews with "drawn features and tired eyes," stationed inside a repurposed school serving as a rest base, waiting for the next callout while a fire that had "calmed down overnight" could "flare up again at any moment." That cumulative fatigue reduces the body's thermoregulatory capacity and delays recognition of early exertional heat stroke symptoms, both in the affected firefighter and among colleagues who should be spotting it.

**Turnout gear**, covering more than 60 percent of body surface area, stays on throughout these extended phases, sharply limiting evaporative cooling exactly when the body needs it most.

## What Cool First, Transport Second demands on this kind of fireground

The **ACSM Expert Consensus Statement 2023** is unambiguous on this point: when exertional heat stroke is suspected, cold water immersion must begin on scene, before transport, with the goal of bringing core body temperature below 39 degrees Celsius within 30 minutes of collapse. Casa et al. (Exercise and Sport Sciences Reviews, 2007) established that this method cools at a rate of 0.35 degrees Celsius per minute, roughly ten times faster than the wet towels or ice packs typically available at a forward command post.

On a fireground like Gironde's, where medical assets can sit several kilometers from the nearest point of engagement, the question is not only whether the right protocol exists, but whether the right equipment is positioned where it can actually be used, immediately. Korey Stringer Institute data, drawn from more than 401 cases treated under this protocol, show a 100 percent survival rate when core temperature drops below 40 degrees Celsius within 30 minutes.
